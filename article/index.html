Benchmark · 15 October 2026 · 60 catalog photos · 7 engines

The free moderation APIs saw nothing. The LLMs saw too much.

We moderated product photos seven ways and measured latency, cost and mistakes on the same images, against labels written before looking at any engine's answer.

Most moderation APIs are built for a social network: is there nudity, violence or gore in this upload? A product catalog that feeds Google, Meta and Pinterest gets listings rejected for different reasons:

  • Is there cannabis or CBD branding on the pack?
  • Is this a smoking product?
  • Does the label claim to cure, treat or prevent a disease?
  • Has someone stamped "SALE" or "free shipping" on the photo?

We asked those five questions of 60 photos from our live catalog, using two fixed-category moderation APIs, four general vision LLMs, and Jev, a System One model. Fifteen photos were picked because they were likely to trip a policy (CBD products, herbal pre-roll joints, libido supplements, a "What Are You Smoking?" BBQ T-shirt); 45 were drawn at random.

Finding 1

The fixed-category APIs returned nothing

Rekognition and omni-moderation returned no labels on any of the 60 photos. That includes six CBD products, all with "CBD" or "THC" printed on the label, and a three-pack of herbal pre-roll joints.

This is not a failure. They do what they were built for. OpenAI's endpoint checks images only for sexual content, violence and self-harm; its drugs category is text-only. Rekognition has a "Drugs & Tobacco" class, and it still did not fire on a tin labelled "Herbal Joints".

If your risk is nudity in user uploads, use omni-moderation: it is free and fast. If your risk is catalog policy, these APIs are not asking your questions, and a clean result from them means nothing.

Finding 2

Flexibility costs latency, and the price range is 50x

Any vision LLM can answer a policy question written in plain English. That is the whole appeal. The costs:

  • Latency. Every GPT model was 2.5–4.5x slower than Jev at the median. The cheapest, gpt-6-luna, had a p95 of 3.2 seconds.
  • Price spread. From $153 to $7,797 per million images, about 50x, between models that caught nearly the same things.
  • Over-reading. The LLMs' false alarms came from reading product names as medical claims ("Healing Salve", "Pain Relief Balm", "Heart Support Tea", "Head Ease"). Three of the four also flagged the BBQ "What Are You Smoking?" T-shirt as a smoking product.

Every GPT model ran at reasoning effort none, its fastest setting. More reasoning might cut the false alarms, but it would add latency and output tokens.

Finding 3

Jev was fastest and cheapest, and it tells you when it is unsure

Jev answered all five questions for $123 per million photos, at 13 photos a second with 8 parallel requests, with one false alarm in 286. It missed one case: a tincture label reading "analgesic … anti-inflammatory", which it scored 0.45. Its one false alarm scored 0.50. Both errors sat at the boundary.

Jev returns a probability for each yes/no question, not a written "true". So we sent every answer between 0.3 and 0.7 to a person:

4 of 294 answers (1.4%) landed in the review band, including both errors. The other 290 were all correct. An LLM returning plain true/false cannot be routed like this. The only way to find its mistakes is to review everything it said yes to.

Where every engine disagreed with us

One photo was flagged as a health claim by every engine: a serum whose label says it "will prevent wrinkles". We labelled that cosmetic, not medical; a regulator might disagree. We kept our label, but this case is about policy, not about any one model. Whatever engine you use, write your definitions down with examples. Jev takes them as criteria; an LLM takes them in the prompt.

What we would do

Product catalog (our case)

Jev with a 0.3–0.7 review band. It is fastest, cheapest at real-time rates, has the fewest false alarms, and it tells us which answers to check.

User uploads

When nudity and violence are the risk, run omni-moderation first. It is free.

Overnight, on a budget

A cheap LLM on the batch tier, accepting more false alarms to review.

Before trusting any number

Label 50 of your own photos before you trust a pricing page, including ours.

Caveats

  • Small sample. 60 photos and 8 positive cases. The gaps in "caught" are one to three photos, which is not statistically meaningful. The false-alarm counts over 286 negatives say more, but are still small. No photo showed nudity or a clear promotional overlay, so those questions measured false alarms only.
  • Labels. One labeller, blind to engine output: Claude, the AI assistant that ran this benchmark, looking at each photo at full resolution. Claude is also an Anthropic model, and Anthropic's Claude Haiku is priced (not measured) above. 6 ambiguous cases were left out of scoring.
  • Clean photos. These are studio shots from curated sellers. User uploads are messier, and every engine will do worse on them.
  • Latency. Measured from one machine on one network, so provider region matters. We did not test rate limits beyond 8 parallel requests.
  • Jev status. Jev's image input is opt-in "Danger Zone": TypeSafe does not yet vouch for it in production.
  • Not measured: Claude, Gemini, Azure, Google Vision, Hive and Sightengine are priced only.
